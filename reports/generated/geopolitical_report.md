### 1. Timeline Correlation

The anomaly peak data aligns precisely with the escalating Iran–US conflict. The top three anomaly days—**April 8 (3,015 anomalies), March 23 (2,512 anomalies), and March 31 (2,425 anomalies)**—correspond directly to critical inflection points in the crisis.

- **March 17–20 (Escalation Cluster):** On March 17, the US bombed Iranian missile silos. The following three trading days—March 18 (1,661 anomalies), March 19 (1,371), and March 20 (2,033)—show a cumulative spike. The jump from 1,371 to 2,033 on March 20 coincides with the UAE joining US military operations, widening the conflict’s geographic scope.
- **March 23 (Peak #2):** This follows the March 19–20 destruction of 16 Iranian commercial vessels and the deployment of A-10s and Apaches. Markets reacted with a one-to-two-day lag as the scale of kinetic engagement became clear.
- **April 8 (Peak #1):** Paradoxically, this is the highest anomaly count (3,015). This date marks the ceasefire announcement. While a relief event, the sheer volume of fund repricing—likely rebalancing out of defensive positions—generated the largest single-day anomaly count.
- **Intervening Days:** March 26 (715), April 1 (697), and April 2 (654) show residual volatility as oil prices stabilized near $126/barrel and the BCB’s Copom statement (late March) warned of Selic rate risk.

### 2. Causality Chain

The transmission mechanism from the Strait of Hormuz to Brazilian fund anomalies is clear:

1. **Strait Closure (Late Feb–March 2):** Iran’s closure removed ~20% of global crude transit, immediately injecting a supply-side risk premium.
2. **Oil Price Spike (Late March):** Brent crude broke $126/barrel, the highest in four years. Brazil’s Petrobras-linked equities and inflation expectations repriced violently.
3. **Global VIX Surge:** The VIX averaged ~20 in the dataset but spiked to 31 on peak days (likely March 18–20). This triggered margin calls and forced redemption in multi-asset funds globally.
4. **USD/BRL Appreciation:** The BRL weakened from ~5.38 (mean) toward 5.74 (max observed) as capital fled to safe havens. A 5.7% depreciation in days compounds losses for BRL-denominated funds.
5. **Ibovespa Selloff:** The Ibovespa fell from a high of ~198,657 to a low of 132,129 (a 33.5% decline over the dataset period). A selloff of this magnitude—concentrated in the March–April window—mechanically triggered anomaly flags in NAV-based fund stress tests.
6. **Fund Anomalies:** The dataset measures deviations from expected returns. A synchronous oil shock + FX crash + equity rout produced an unprecedented 12.1% to 38.8% anomaly rate in the largest fund class (CLASSES - FIF) on peak dates.

### 3. Sector Impact

- **CLASSES - FIF (avg anomaly 3.6%, peak 38.8% on March 3):** This is the dominant fund type (24,886 funds). The peak on March 3 reflects the initial shock of the Strait closure. The sustained high anomaly rate (10–38% through March 16) indicates these multi-asset funds were most exposed to the full causality chain—both oil-linked equities and BRL-denominated fixed income.
- **CLASSE FIF/FAPI (avg anomaly 3.4%, peak 33.3% on March 12 and 20):** These are smaller but highly concentrated. The 33.3% peak on March 12 (pre–US bombing) suggests early stress in structured credit or real estate funds tied to oil-dependent sectors. Another 33.3% on March 20 aligns with the UAE entry into the conflict.
- **FI (avg anomaly 2.5%, peak 33.3% on Feb 13, Apr 9, Apr 17):** These are niche funds (only 3–7 total). The April 9 and April 17 anomalies reflect post-ceasefire idiosyncratic adjustments, not broad market stress.

### 4. Ceasefire Effect

The **April 8 ceasefire** is the most complex signal in the data. It recorded **3,015 anomalies**—the highest of any day—but this is **likely a relief event, not a panic**. The anomaly detection algorithm probably flagged the mass repositioning from defensive oil/gold/currency hedges back into risk assets. Funds that had been holding elevated cash or short-BRL positions reversed them in a single day, generating statistical outliers. By April 10 (1,024 anomalies), the anomaly count dropped sharply, confirming that April 8 was a one-time repricing event.

### 5. BCB / Copom Dimension

The Brazilian Central Bank’s explicit mention of the conflict as a Selic risk factor introduced a **second-order fixed-income shock**. The dataset shows a US 10-year yield range of 3.95% to 4.60% during the crisis, but Brazil’s Selic-linked bonds saw steeper moves. The Copom statement (late March) signaled that the conflict’s inflation pass-through might delay rate cuts. For **CLASSES - FIF** funds holding long-duration Brazilian government bonds, this repriced yields upward, generating anomalies that persisted through March 26 (715 anomalies) and April 1–2 (697 and 654 anomalies). Fixed-income funds, typically lower volatility, were pushed into anomaly status by BCB forward guidance.

### 6. Conclusion

This episode reveals that Brazilian investment funds are highly sensitive to Middle East geopolitical shocks through **three cascading channels**: commodity price transmission (oil), global risk appetite (VIX/BRL), and domestic monetary policy (BCB/Copom). The **CLASSES - FIF** fund type, representing over 24,000 funds, is the primary transmission belt—absorbing both equity and fixed-income shocks. The anomaly data shows that (1) escalation events produce immediate 2–3 day spikes; (2) the ceasefire generated more anomalies than the conflict itself due to mass repositioning; and (3) fixed-income funds exhibited a delayed but persistent anomaly pattern as BCB adjusted its policy stance. **For EM fund managers, hedging Brazilian exposure against Middle East risk is not optional—it is a structural requirement.** The Strait of Hormuz closure proved that a single chokepoint can generate systemic anomalies in a distant emerging market within 48 hours.
